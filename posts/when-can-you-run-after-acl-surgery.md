Before almost anything else, I asked my surgeon one question: when can I run again?

I wanted a date. A week number I could count down to and circle in my calendar. What I slowly worked out, and what nobody says plainly enough, is that the date was never the right thing to ask about.

## The calendar is the wrong question

Search around and you'll see the same numbers. Twelve weeks. Three to four months. Some people quote sixteen weeks.

Those are averages, and averages hide a lot. Your graft, your strength, your swelling and your age all move that number around. Two people who had the same operation on the same day can be weeks apart in whether their knee is ready to take the load of running.

And running before it's ready is the single fastest way to set yourself back. Too early, too fast, and you're inviting the pain, swelling and setbacks you spent months avoiding.

So the useful question isn't "how many weeks has it been?" It's "does my knee pass the tests yet?"

## The signs your knee is actually ready

Running is a series of small single-leg landings, over and over, each one loading the knee with more than your bodyweight. Before you start, the knee needs to handle that. Physios tend to look for a few things:

- Your operated knee bends and straightens as well as your other one, with nothing held back.
- The joint is calm. The swelling has settled, and it isn't puffy or warm after a normal day.
- Your quad strength is close to your good side. This is the big one. A common benchmark is getting the operated leg to around 80% or more of the other leg's strength, measured properly rather than eyeballed.
- Pain is low and steady, not a knee that flares every time you push it.
- You can do single-leg work and small hops, land softly, and the knee doesn't grumble the next day.

None of these are things to guess at. Your physio can measure the ones you can't feel, especially quad strength, which almost always lags further behind than it feels like it should.

## What ready actually felt like

For me, the shift wasn't dramatic. There was no single morning where the knee announced it was done.

It was more that the small things stopped being events. Stairs became boring. A long walk didn't leave the knee swollen that evening. Single-leg squats went from a wobble to something I could control. When I finally jogged for one minute, it felt almost anticlimactic, which is exactly how it should feel. If your first run feels like a leap of faith, you probably went too soon.

## How the walk-to-run progression works

Once you're cleared, you don't just go for a run. You build up to one.

The whole thing is walk and run intervals, and it starts smaller than you'd expect:

- Begin with one-minute runs, each with a walk in between to recover.
- Add reps before you add length. More one-minute runs before you try a two-minute one.
- Over a few weeks the runs stretch out and the walking shrinks, until the walking drops away and you're running continuously for 20, then 30 minutes.

The pace is easy and the ground is flat and soft. Three or four outings a week, no more than four to six, and you fill the gaps with cycling or aqua-jogging so you keep your fitness without pounding the joint.

## The one rule that keeps it safe

Let symptoms decide, not the calendar and not your training partner.

If a session felt hard, or the knee is swollen or sore the next morning, you repeat that step or drop back one. You never force the next jump because a plan told you to. The progression bends to your knee, not the other way around.

This is where a running buddy or an old training plan will lead you wrong. They're pulling you toward a date. Your knee is the only thing that actually knows.

One honest caveat: this isn't medical advice, and I'm not your physio. Clear your own return to running with your surgeon or physiotherapist first. They can measure the things you can't feel, and they know your knee's history. Use this to ask better questions at your next appointment, not to skip it.

You will run again. The knee just needs to tell you it's ready first, and if you keep doing the work, it will.
